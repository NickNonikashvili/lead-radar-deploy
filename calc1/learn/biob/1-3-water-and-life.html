1.3 Water and life

Study notes for Water and life in BIOB 160 Principles of Living Systems at Montana State University: the big ideas, key formulas, a worked example, common mistakes and an exam tip. The same topic has endless practice questions and flashcards in Mathub.

Big ideas

  • Water is polar: oxygen carries a partial negative charge and the hydrogens partial positive charges, so each molecule can hydrogen-bond with up to four neighbors.
  • Hydrogen bonding explains cohesion (water sticks to itself, giving surface tension and helping water move up plants) and adhesion (water sticks to other surfaces).
  • Water moderates temperature: high specific heat (1 cal/g·°C) and high heat of vaporization give evaporative cooling. Ice floats because its hydrogen bonds hold molecules farther apart, so it is less dense than liquid water.
  • Water is a versatile solvent for ions and polar molecules (hydrophilic); nonpolar molecules are hydrophobic. Concentration is measured in molarity (moles per liter).
  • pH = −log[H⁺]. Each unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺]; pH 7 is neutral, below 7 acidic, above 7 basic, and [H⁺][OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴. Buffers resist pH changes (the carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer in blood). Rising CO₂ makes oceans more acidic.

Key formulas

pH
$$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$$
Water constant
$$[\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 10^{-14}$$

Worked example

Solution A has pH 3 and solution B has pH 6. How many times more H⁺ does A have? What is [OH⁻] in solution B?
Show the solution
Each pH unit is a factor of 10, so A has 10³ = 1,000 times more H⁺. In B, [H⁺] = 10⁻⁶ M, so [OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴ / 10⁻⁶ = 10⁻⁸ M.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a lower pH means fewer H⁺. Lower pH means more H⁺ (more acidic).
  • Treating the pH scale as linear. A change of 2 units is a 100-fold change.
  • Saying ice floats because it has more hydrogen bonds by weight. It floats because stable hydrogen bonds space molecules farther apart.
Exam tipReading: Campbell ch. 3 (pp. 44–55). Practise pH and [H⁺] conversions with powers of ten; they show up every year.
