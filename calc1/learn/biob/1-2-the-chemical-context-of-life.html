1.2 The chemical context of life

Study notes for The chemical context of life in BIOB 160 Principles of Living Systems at Montana State University: the big ideas, key formulas, a worked example, common mistakes and an exam tip. The same topic has endless practice questions and flashcards in Mathub.

Big ideas

  • About 96% of living matter is C, H, O and N. An atom has protons and neutrons in the nucleus and electrons in shells; the atomic number is the number of protons and the mass number is protons + neutrons.
  • Isotopes have the same number of protons but different neutrons. Radioactive isotopes decay at a fixed half-life (carbon-14 about 5,730 years) and are used as tracers and for dating.
  • Chemical behavior depends on valence electrons in the outer shell. Atoms form bonds to fill it: H makes 1 bond, O 2, N 3, C 4.
  • Covalent bonds share electrons: nonpolar when shared equally, polar when one atom is more electronegative (O and N pull hard). Ionic bonds form between ions with opposite charges after an electron is transferred (Na⁺Cl⁻).
  • Weak bonds shape biology: hydrogen bonds (a partially positive H attracted to an electronegative O or N), van der Waals interactions and ionic attractions in water. Molecular shape determines function, as with enzymes, receptors and drugs.

Key formulas

Mass number
$$A = \text{protons} + \text{neutrons}$$

Worked example

Oxygen has atomic number 8. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does a neutral atom of oxygen-18 have, and how many covalent bonds does oxygen usually make?
Show the solution
8 protons (atomic number), 10 neutrons (18 − 8) and 8 electrons (neutral). With 6 valence electrons it needs 2 more, so it usually forms 2 covalent bonds.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up atomic number (protons) and mass number (protons + neutrons).
  • Calling a hydrogen bond a covalent bond. It is a weak attraction between molecules or parts of a molecule.
  • Forgetting that a polar covalent bond is unequal sharing, not a transfer of electrons.
Exam tipReading: Campbell ch. 2 (pp. 28–43). Rank bond strength: covalent > ionic (in water, ionic bonds are weakened) > hydrogen > van der Waals.
