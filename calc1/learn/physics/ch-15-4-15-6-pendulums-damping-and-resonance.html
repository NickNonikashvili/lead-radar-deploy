Ch 15.4–15.6 Pendulums, damping, and resonance

Study notes for Pendulums, damping, and resonance in PHSX 220 Physics I (w/ Calculus) at Montana State University: the big ideas, key formulas, a worked example, common mistakes and an exam tip. The same topic has endless practice questions and flashcards in MatHub.

Big ideas

  • Simple pendulum (small angles): $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, independent of mass and (for small swings) amplitude. Physical pendulum: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I/(mgd)}$ with $d$ from pivot to center of mass.
  • Measuring $g$: time many swings, $g = 4\pi^2L/T^2$. Longer pendulum, longer period; on the Moon the period grows.
  • Damping: friction or drag makes the amplitude decay exponentially; the frequency drops slightly. Underdamped oscillates, critically damped returns fastest without oscillating, overdamped creeps back.
  • Forced oscillations and resonance: driving at the natural frequency $\omega_0$ produces the largest amplitude (pushing a swing, shattering glass, Tacoma Narrows). Less damping makes the resonance peak sharper and taller.

Key formulas

Pendulums
$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac Lg}\ \text{(simple)},\qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgd}}\ \text{(physical)}$$
Damped amplitude
$$x(t) = A_0 e^{-bt/2m}\cos(\omega' t + \phi)$$

Worked example

What length pendulum has a period of 2.0 s on Earth?
Show the solution
$L = gT^2/(4\pi^2) = 9.8(4)/39.5 = 0.993$ m: the classic "seconds pendulum".

Common mistakes

  • Using the pendulum formula for a large-angle swing (it is a small-angle approximation).
  • Assuming a heavier bob swings slower.
Exam tipFor pendulum questions, ask what changes L or g. Nothing else matters (at small angles).
